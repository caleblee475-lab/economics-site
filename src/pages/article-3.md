## Executive Summary

1. **Structural Economic Vulnerabilities:** Singapore's open and import-dependent economy makes it vulnerable to supply disruptions and cost-push inflation, albeit with time lags as businesses adjust to higher costs.
2. **Flexible Policy Mechanisms:** By strengthening the dollar, MAS cools global demand for exports and mitigates imported inflation via a steeper S$NEER, combating both demand-pull and cost-push inflation respectively.
3. **Continued Export Competitiveness:** Singapore's integration within the Global Value Chain and its high-complexity products dampen export price elasticities, preserving export competitiveness and mitigating the countereffect of currency appreciation.

## Singapore's Economic Vulnerabilities

As an open economy with total import and export volume worth more than triple of its GDP [1] and domestic consumption overwhelmingly dependent on imports, Singapore's economy is vulnerable to imported inflation from external supply shocks. With local production chains relying heavily on imported intermediate inputs and foreign goods in exports and production, any form of supply disruption would raise costs and disrupt production and trade through longer trade routes, increased fuel and shipping costs or unforeseen delays.

Given Singapore's heavy reliance on imported consumption goods and intermediate inputs, external price shocks would be expected to place upward pressure on consumer prices, as reflected in a rise in the Consumer Price Index (CPI). However, the observed effects of cost-push inflation and inflated import prices do not immediately translate into an increase in the CPI as the increase has to be filtered through importers, wholesalers and retailers before reaching the consumer. Hence, any increases from cost-push and imported inflation are only gradually reflected in the CPI over several quarters. This is consistently shown by comparing the CPI against the global commodity price index (PALLFNFINDEXQ).

One recent illustration is the raising of electricity and gas tariffs by 17% and 7.1% respectively in July 2026, the first significant increase since the closure of the Straits of Hormuz in February, nearly 4 months apart. While oil prices spiked upwards since March with Brent crude oil jumping from USD$71.33 to a high of USD$115.62 per barrel in May, consumer energy prices remained stable locally as firms used stockpiles and continued honoring pre-crisis contracts, adjusting retail tariffs after existing contracts and stockpiles were exhausted.

## Tackling Inflation: Global Economic Shocks and Supply Chain Disruptions

Recent CPI increases in Singapore have reflected both demand-pull and cost-push pressures. Though the two follow distinct macroeconomic causes and mechanisms, it is not uncommon for both to occur at once, exacerbating upward price pressures as more consumers globally compete for a shrinking supply. However, past examples suggest that an appreciation of the SGD via a steeper S$NEER by the Monetary Authority of Singapore (MAS) has helped to moderate both types of inflationary pressures.

### The Post-Pandemic Era: A Resurgence in Spending

As the global economy gradually opened with mass vaccinations and the acute effects of the pandemic passed, 'pent-up' demand by consumers both local and foreign led to a surge of consumer spending domestically and abroad, fuelled further by capital accrued from savings during the pandemic's lockdown periods where typical spending habits were inaccessible.

Unlike consumers, suppliers and firms were still in the nascent stages of resuming production, hence the spike in aggregate demand was met with insufficient supply. This resulted in price spikes as firms charged higher prices for limited commodities, leading to demand-pull inflation.

By allowing the Singapore dollar to appreciate, MAS reduced imported inflation and dampened external demand for Singapore's exports, helping to moderate overall price pressures. Weaker external demand would also tend to soften production activity and derived demand for intermediate inputs, allowing the economy to cool and the market to stabilise. [3]

### Inflation Today: The Straits of Hormuz

Recent disruptions to shipping through the Strait of Hormuz have raised global energy and related import costs, posing renewed cost-push inflation risks. With petroleum refining being a significant domestic industry and 95% of our electricity derived from imported natural gas [4], this poses an upside risk to domestic costs and prices.

Due to the evolving and uncertain situation, MAS has signalled that imported cost pressures are likely to remain elevated in the near term. As a key production and transport input, higher energy costs would tend to feed into broader consumer prices over time. Hence, the CPI would tend to rise in tandem with electricity and fuel prices.

To combat cost-push and imported inflation from disrupted supply of input and raw goods such as crude oil and natural gas, MAS steepened the S$NEER policy band and allowed the dollar to appreciate. This mitigated the magnitude of import cost increases and the resulting inflationary effects. [5,6]

## Currency Strength and the CPI: The Exchange Rate Pass-Through Effect

Given Singapore's identified systemic vulnerabilities to external supply shocks, historical data unexpectedly reveals a delayed and muted increase in the CPI in response to global commodity price increases. Though changes are observed over time, the time lag spans several quarters and the percentage changes considerably less. This is tied to the Exchange Rate Pass-Through (ERPT) effect, where changes in external prices are softened and deferred as they translate into the domestic economy.

External price shocks are not immediately seen in CPI changes, but go through a 2-stage process. The first stage affects the costs of imported raw materials and intermediary goods at the border. Then, the price increases go through the second stage as the costs filter through wholesalers, retailers, and logistical service providers before impacting consumer prices. This explains the delay present in Figure 1. The ERPT is one of the main causes behind the effectiveness of Singapore's currency appreciation policy; strengthening the S$NEER helps to moderate price increases across both stages of the process. [6]

In the first stage, a stronger SGD lowers Singapore dollar import costs for firms, directly mitigating the immediate inflationary effects of global supply shocks at the border. This directly counteracts cost-push inflation for domestic consumers and producers importing input goods. Naturally, goods CPI responds roughly two months faster than services CPI due to the former's direct consumption of imported goods and the latter being tied to fixed factors such as rent and labor contracts that take time to adjust.

As the adjusted import prices are translated into the CPI at the second stage, the effect is weakened by domestic factors. For consumers, retail prices are moderated by non-tradable domestic costs such as rent and labor, which remain stable and unaffected, creating a fixed cost floor that diminishes the marginal impact of a stronger currency. Furthermore, retailers often account for and absorb external price volatility, further lowering the proportionate effect that external supply shocks have on consumer prices and spending. Domestic labour and other non-tradable costs can further offset part of the disinflationary effect of a stronger currency, so the reduction in CPI is smaller than the initial reduction in import prices [5,6].

Because exchange-rate appreciation lowers imported input costs at the border, it reduces the inflation impulse before it enters domestic supply chains, and the remaining impulse is diluted further as it passes through distribution and retail margins. [7] As such, the ERPT is never on a 1:1 ratio; approximately 60% of the long-run CPI impact of an external price shock is seen only by the end of the second year after its inception.

A stronger S$NEER can help moderate core inflation over time through a compounding pass-through effect, even if core inflation does not fall immediately in the same quarters. It directly reduces the upward pressure of supply shocks on the import costs in the Singapore dollar. This mitigated increase is further diluted as it passes through the local supply chain, leading to an even lower, sub-proportional rate of increase in the CPI. The concurrent rise in core inflation and the SGD's appreciation rate observed in Figures 2 and 3 does not contradict the argument as MAS typically tightens in response to inflationary pressures. Hence, the effect of appreciation on CPI is moderated and gradual, aiming to slow inflation rather than halt it entirely.

## Maintaining Export Competitiveness

Exchange-rate tightening can reduce inflation, but it also creates trade-offs for globally exposed sectors. Given that external demand contributed around 67-72% of Singapore's GDP in 2015 to 2019, a stronger S$NEER is expected to impact the price competitiveness of export-oriented sectors by softening foreign demand for their output. [11] However, the policy's drawbacks appear limited in scale due to two alleviating factors governing the nature of Singapore's exports.

### Global Value Chain Integration

As a global refinery and processing node, approximately 61.5% of Singapore's merchandise exports are re-exports, making up S$454.8B of a total of S$739.4B, and are mostly intermediary goods within the Global Value Chain (GVC). [1,12] Given Singapore's industrial specialty in high-tech, refining, advanced manufacturing and computing, a large proportion of export values come from the raw intermediary goods imported. With a stronger S$NEER lowering input costs for local firms, currency appreciation can offset cost pressure in high-import-content exports, maintaining price competitiveness in trade despite the Singapore dollar's higher value.

### Export Complexity and Inherent Price Inelasticities

Singapore's advanced industrial and technological base exports many relatively complex products, and higher economic complexity is associated with lower export price elasticity, so price increases from a stronger local currency may not produce proportionally large declines in quantity demanded for some export sectors. This effect may be especially relevant in sectors where Singapore specializes in high-end intermediate goods or advanced services, including electronics [13], aerospace [14], and parts of biomedical production [15].

Hence, due to Singapore's high-value, high-complexity exports, its integration into the GVC and the trade structure of its exports appears to have a dampening effect on price elasticities, rendering them less sensitive to Real Effective Exchange Rate (REER) appreciations. [1]

Thus, the exchange rate policy of currency appreciation via strengthening the S$NEER offers considerable mitigatory benefits for Singapore in terms of combatting both demand-pull and cost-push inflation from affecting the CPI, while the unique nature of its exports and position within the GVC suggest that the adverse effect on export performance may be dampened.
